**Short answer:** almost every construction dispute was seeded in the contract. The clauses that cause the most trouble later — scope, change orders, notice, time bars, and flow-downs — are the ones worth reading carefully *before* you sign, because they decide who is entitled to what, and they are rarely negotiable once the work is underway.

This is general information, not legal advice; have counsel review your specific contract. But the following patterns are where disputes are born.

## 1. Vague scope and undefined exclusions

The single most common seed of a payment dispute is a scope that was never defined clearly. If "the work" doesn't have firm boundaries, then extra work — and who pays for it — becomes a fight. Look for:

- Undefined or "to be determined" scope items.
- Exclusions that are listed but not specific.
- "Incidental work" language that's broad enough to swallow real change orders.

## 2. A weak or missing change-order procedure

A project that changes but has no clear mechanism for pricing and approving changes is a project that will argue about money later. You want to know, before you start: How is extra work identified? Who prices it? What's the process and the deadline? An informal "we'll figure it out" approach is a future dispute.

## 3. Notice requirements that are strict

Many contracts require **written notice** within a fixed window to preserve a claim or a change request. The risk isn't that the event is invalid — it's that the **deadline** was missed. Notice clauses read as technicalities until they're the reason a claim is barred.

## 4. Time bars

Related to notice, a **time bar** deems a claim waived after a set period. Because these run on the contract's own clock, the calendar of deadlines matters as much as the facts of the project. A strong claim with a missed time bar can still fail.

## 5. Payment terms, retainage, and back-charges

Disputes cluster around: retainage percentage and release timing, the basis for payment applications, lien rights, and **back-charges** (deductions a party takes without a clear contractual basis). Ambiguity here creates cash-flow fights that can escalate into formal disputes.

## 6. Flow-down inconsistencies

On larger projects the prime's contract is "flowed down" to subcontractors. If the flow-down is inconsistent — a subcontract that doesn't match the prime on notice, time bars, or scope — it creates gaps where two parties assume the other is covered. Mismatches are a frequent source of allocation disputes.

## 7. One-sided "as directed" or unlimited obligations

Language like "as the architect directs" or open-ended cooperation obligations can create expectations that are hard to price and hard to defend when the other side invokes them.

## 8. An unclear (or missing) dispute-resolution procedure

Knowing *how* a dispute will be handled — mediation, arbitration, litigation, and in what order — affects strategy and cost. A missing or contradictory procedure is its own problem once the relationship breaks down.

## The habit that prevents most of these

Contemporaneous records. The parties who win entitlement disputes are usually the ones who kept clear, dated records of scope, changes, notices, and communications *as the project happened* — not reconstructed afterward.

If a contract is already in place and things are going sideways, the earlier you understand what you're entitled to under its notice and change clauses, the more options you keep.

**Short answer:** in a defect or performance dispute, the question is rarely "does it look fine?" It's "does the work meet the applicable building code, the contract and plans, and the recognized standard of care?" Compliance analysis is where a lot of construction disputes are won or lost — and a handful of building systems account for most of the failures.

This is general information, not legal or engineering advice. But understanding where work commonly falls short is useful whether you're an owner, a contractor, or counsel evaluating a matter.

## What "compliance" actually means

Compliance is measured against more than one thing:

- The **applicable building code** — the **IRC** for residential work and the **IBC** for commercial work, plus any local amendments.
- The **contract, plans, and specifications** for the project.
- **Component and material standards** — for example, **ASTM** requirements for materials and **ACI** guidance for reinforced concrete.
- **Manufacturer installation instructions** for specific components.
- The **recognized standard of care** for the trade.

A single component can fail one of these while meeting the others — which is why "it passed inspection" is not the same as "it was done to standard."

## Where construction most often falls short

### The building envelope and water management
The single most common source of claims. The code treats the building envelope as a system — a continuous **water-resistive barrier** and **drainage plane**, proper **flashing**, and continuity of the **air barrier**. Gaps, mislapped flashing, or a broken envelope continuity let water in even when individual components are installed "to spec."

### Structural and foundation work
Where it fails, it tends to be serious: **loads** not matching the design, **reinforced concrete** that doesn't meet **ACI** requirements (cover, reinforcement, placement), inadequate **footings** or foundations, and connections that don't match the structural drawings.

### Roofing and drainage
Roofing assembly continuity, proper **slope and drainage** to avoid ponding, and flashing at penetrations and edges. A roof that meets the material specification but is detailed incorrectly can still fail.

### Energy, insulation, and air barrier
Insulation values, continuous **air-barrier** performance, and thermal-break requirements. These are easy to get wrong at interfaces — corners, transitions, and penetrations — and increasingly scrutinized.

### Egress, fire separation, and life safety
Door hardware, clear egress widths, **fire-rated separations**, and the performance of rated assemblies. A rated wall that is compromised by an uncoordinated opening can fail its separation function.

### Materials that don't match the specification
Substitutions and product deviations — using a material that doesn't meet the specified **ASTM** properties, or a product not listed/approved for its use. "It works" is not the same as "it conforms."

## How compliance is assessed in a dispute

A defensible compliance analysis runs in a disciplined order:

1. **Establish the standard** — the correct code edition, the plans, and the applicable component standards.
2. **Document the actual work** — inspection, the field conditions, and the evidence.
3. **Compare** the work to each applicable requirement.
4. **Determine the standard of care** and whether the work met it.
5. **Quantify** where the deficiency creates a cost to correct or a performance risk.

The through-line is the same as in every expert opinion: tie the conclusion to a named standard, show the reasoning, and be prepared to defend it to a fact-finder.

**Short answer:** a defensible expert witness report does more than reach a conclusion — it shows the path. It frames the issues, lists what was reviewed, grounds the analysis in the contract, the plans, the codes, and recognized standards, and then states a clear, reasoned opinion. If a judge, jury, or arbitrator can't follow the reasoning, the conclusion doesn't matter.

The report is the expert's first appearance in the matter. Here are the sections a strong report contains, in the order they should build on one another.

## 1. Scope and issues

The report opens by defining the issues the expert has been asked to address, drawn from the complaint and answer. It states what is in scope and, importantly, what is **not** — any reservations or limitations on the assignment. Vague scope is the root of a lot of later impeachment.

## 2. Qualifications

A concise statement of the credentials that bear on these specific issues — licensure, education, and directly relevant experience — with the full curriculum vitae attached. The goal is to connect the qualifications to the issues, not to pad the CV.

## 3. Materials reviewed

An itemized record of what the expert actually relied on: the contract and change orders, architectural and engineering plans, permits and inspection records, photographs and video, correspondence, schedules, and cost records. If the opinion rests on a document, the document should be identifiable here.

## 4. Inspection and observations

A description of any site visit — what was observed, when, and under what conditions. This section is what separates a grounded opinion from a paper one. Opposing counsel will almost always ask whether the expert looked at the work.

## 5. Applicable standards

The report names the yardsticks the work is measured against: the **contract, plans, and specifications**, the **applicable building codes**, and **recognized industry standards** such as the IRC, IBC, and the relevant ASTM or ACI guidance. Naming the standard is what makes the later opinion testable.

## 6. Analysis and findings

This is the analytical core — and the place where reports most often break down. The key move is to address **causation**, not just condition. A report that says "this wall is leaking" is a condition; a report that says "the flashing was installed contrary to the manufacturer's instructions and the IBC, which is the probable cause of the intrusion" is a finding.

## 7. Opinion

A clear, usually numbered, opinion on each issue — tied to the standards above. Where relevant, the opinion quantifies: the **cost to correct**, or the **schedule and productivity impact**. Each opinion should be traceable back to the analysis and the standards.

## 8. Assumptions, limitations, and reservations

What the opinion depends on, what the expert did not have access to, and any conditions that would change the conclusion. Disclosing limitations strengthens credibility rather than weakening the opinion.

## 9. Exhibits

Photographs, drawings, schedule clips, and cost tables that support each finding, organized so a fact-finder can match each exhibit to the opinion it supports.

## The weaknesses to watch for

- Conclusions stated without the reasoning that supports them.
- No reference to codes or recognized standards.
- No site inspection.
- Causation missing — condition described but cause not established.
- Undisclosed assumptions.

A report built the way above is one your side can defend and a fact-finder can rely on.

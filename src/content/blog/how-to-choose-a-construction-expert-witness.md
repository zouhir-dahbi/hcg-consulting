**Short answer:** retain an expert whose qualifications match your issues, who is genuinely independent, and whose opinions are tied to the contract, the plans, the codes, and recognized standards — not an expert who simply "sounds impressive." The right expert makes your case; the wrong one becomes a cross-examination target.

Choosing a construction expert witness is one of the highest-leverage retention decisions in a construction dispute. The expert you select will define how the trier of fact understands causation, standard of care, and damages. Here is the checklist I would apply if I were selecting on the other side of the table.

## 1. Verify qualifications against the specific issues

Don't ask "is this a credible expert?" Ask "is this expert qualified to opine on *these* issues?"

- For **structural, envelope, or code-compliance** opinions: a licensed Professional Engineer, ideally with a Ph.D. or advanced degree in construction management or civil engineering, is usually essential.
- For **schedule and delay** opinions: look for project-controls and CPM experience (time impact analysis, measured-mile methods).
- For **cost** opinions: look for cost estimation and cost-analysis experience tied to the contract.

Confirm the expert is **admissible in your jurisdiction**. A Virginia or D.C. matter benefits from an expert already admitted in those court systems — it reduces friction on qualification.

## 2. Confirm genuine independence

The most damaging cross-examination theme is "the expert has a stake in the outcome." Prefer an expert whose role is **strictly evaluative** — who does not perform the repair work, has no subcontractor or vendor relationships on the project, and is comfortable rendering an opinion even where it complicates the retention. Independence is what lets a judge or jury accept the conclusion.

## 3. Judge report quality on substance

Ask to see the approach, not a marketing brochure. A strong report:

- Frames the issues from the **complaint, contract, and plans**, not from a conclusion.
- Identifies **causation**, not just condition — *why* the work failed, not merely *that* it failed.
- Measures the work against the **applicable building codes** and **recognized industry standards** (IRC, IBC, ASTM, ACI) so the opinion "holds up under scrutiny."
- Quantifies where needed — **cost to correct**, or the schedule and productivity impact.

## 4. Weigh deposition and trial experience

A report is only the start. You want an expert who has **sat for depositions and testified at trial**, and who can explain a technical point in plain English to a non-technical fact-finder. Ask directly: how many matters have you testified on, in which jurisdictions, and on which side?

## 5. Test communication early

Send a redacted sample of the record and ask the expert to outline, in plain terms, how they would structure the opinion and what additional documents they would need. A good expert will tell you where the record is strong *and* where it's thin — including where the other side has a point. An expert who only confirms your theory is a risk.

## Red flags

- "Checkbox" reports and boilerplate conclusions with no case-specific analysis.
- No site visit or inspection of the actual work.
- Over-promising an outcome or "guaranteeing" the case.
- Unwillingness to consider the opposing party's position.
- Vague on qualifications, or unable to point to court admissions.

## Next step

The fastest way to evaluate fit is to review the actual matter. Share a brief and the key documents, and a qualified expert will advise on scope, the strength of the record, and next steps before you commit.

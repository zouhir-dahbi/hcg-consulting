**Short answer:** a delay claim is only as good as the method behind it. The industry-standard approach is to start from the contract (baseline) schedule, identify the delay events, and use **time impact analysis** to show which of those events moved the critical path. For **productivity loss**, the **measured-mile** method isolates the loss by comparing what the crew actually did on unaffected work against what the schedule planned.

Delay and disruption claims are among the most technical issues in a construction dispute — and the analysis method is where credibility is won or lost. Here is the framework, in plain terms.

## The building blocks

- **Baseline schedule.** The contract schedule — the plan the project was measured against. Without a usable baseline, a delay claim has no reference point.
- **Critical path.** The longest sequence of activities that sets the finish date. Only critical-path delay extends completion; everything else has **float**.
- **Float.** The slack an activity has before it affects the finish. Understanding whose float was consumed is central to delay analysis.
- **Excusable vs. non-excusable delay.** Excusable delay is caused by events the contractor is not responsible for (often owner-caused or excusable conditions); non-excusable delay is the contractor's own. **Concurrent delay** is when both occur at once — a frequent point of contention on entitlement.

## Time Impact Analysis (TIA)

TIA is the widely used method for evaluating how a specific delay event affects the schedule. In essence, it:

1. Starts from the **updated schedule** at the time the delay event occurred.
2. **Inserts** the delay event at the correct point and logic.
3. **Re-calculates** the schedule to show the effect on the critical path and the finish date.
4. **Isolates** the impact attributable to that event, separate from other delays.

Done properly, TIA ties a particular event — a late drawing, a RFI, an out-of-sequence work directive — to a specific, quantified effect on completion. It is favored in practice and by the industry's scheduling guidance (such as the AACE's recommended practices) precisely because it is transparent and auditable.

## The Measured-Mile method (productivity loss)

Delay is not the only disruption. A crew can be on time but **slowed down** by out-of-sequence work, congestion, or site conditions — a loss of **productivity**. The measured-mile method quantifies that:

1. Identify a portion of the work that was performed **without** the disruptive conditions — the "mile."
2. Measure the crew's **actual productivity** on that section.
3. Compare it to the **planned productivity** from the baseline or the contract.
4. Apply that productivity ratio to the affected work to estimate the **productivity loss**.

The method's strength is that it uses the contractor's own unaffected performance as the benchmark, rather than an industry average that the other side can attack.

## What a defensible cost-and-delay analysis includes

- The **contract and baseline schedule**, and how it is to be read.
- The **actuals** — as-built schedule, updated schedules, field records.
- A clear list of the **delay events** and who caused each.
- The **method** (TIA for delay, measured-mile for productivity) and why it fits the project.
- A transparent **quantification** — the extension of time and, where applicable, the cost impact.
- The **assumptions and limitations** the analysis depends on.

The goal is the same in cost and delay as in any expert opinion: a finding a trier of fact can follow, grounded in the contract and the record.

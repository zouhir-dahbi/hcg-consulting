**Short answer:** a construction defect claim lives or dies on documentation. Capture the condition, the standard it should have met, the timeline, the physical evidence, and the clues to cause — early, and before conditions change. A well-documented defect is a claim; an undocumented one is an argument.

Most defect problems are not discovered all at once. They appear as a stain, a crack, a draft, or a claim from a resident. What determines whether it becomes a strong claim is what you do in the days and weeks after that first sign.

## 1. Document the condition — and the standard it failed

A defect is only a defect measured against something. Record:

- **What is wrong**, in plain, factual terms (location, extent, what you can see).
- **The standard the work should have met**: the contract, plans, and specifications; the applicable **building codes** (IRC for residential, IBC for commercial); and any **manufacturer installation instructions** that apply to the component.

The gap between "this is what happened" and "this is what was required" is the heart of the claim.

## 2. Nail down the timeline

Dates drive causation and preserve credibility. Record:

- When the problem **first appeared** and how it has progressed.
- The **sequence of related work** — which trades, in what order, when.
- **Weather or seasonal events** that preceded or followed (a leak that follows a specific storm is a different story from a slow, seasonal one).
- Any **notices** you gave, and when.

## 3. Capture physical evidence

- **Dated photographs and video** from multiple angles, including context shots that show location.
- **Measurements** (crack widths, deflection, moisture readings).
- Any **testing** that is appropriate — moisture content, structural or material testing — done by the right person.
- Preserve **samples and materials** where relevant.

Evidence that is taken once, early, and labeled with dates is worth far more than a recollection six months later.

## 4. Preserve the record and the correspondence

Save the contract, change orders, plans, inspection reports, warranties, and all **correspondence** about the problem. Do not delete. In a dispute, documents can be discoverable, and an absent record can be read against you. If you are or may become a party to litigation, treat all potentially relevant materials as things to **preserve**.

## 5. Keep cost and scope separate from cause

Get an **independent estimate** of the cost to correct, and describe the scope of the correction. But keep that distinct from the causation question. A clear scope and number support the damages side of the claim; the cause is a separate, expert-level question.

## Common mistakes that weaken a defect claim

- **Delayed documentation** — waiting too long, so conditions change.
- **No photographs** at the time of discovery.
- **Conflating design and workmanship** without a clear analysis of which standard failed.
- **Hastily repairing** before the evidence is captured, destroying the record of cause.
- **Assuming the photos "tell the story"** without tying them to the governing plans and codes.

## When to bring in an expert

Early. A qualified expert can tell you what to document, guide the inspection, and separate design from workmanship — before positions harden and evidence disappears. The earlier the expert is involved, the stronger and cheaper the path to a defensible opinion.
